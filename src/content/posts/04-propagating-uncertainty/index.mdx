A laser scanner reports that a landmark is 2 m away, straight ahead, with a range error of about 10 cm and a bearing error of about $25^\circ$. Where is the landmark in Cartesian coordinates, and how uncertain is that position? The range and bearing are Gaussian, but the conversion $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ bends space, and the uncertainty bends with it. Below, 2000 samples of the reading are pushed through the conversion.

<NonlinearPropagation client:visible />

The cloud of output samples is not an ellipse. It is a curved band, a "banana", because a bearing error swings the point along a circle around the sensor. The dashed ellipse is what the standard shortcut, linearization, predicts. It is centred too far out, it is too thin, and only 70% of the samples fall inside what it claims is a 95% region.

## Intuition

Uncertainty never stays put. Each time a robot moves, converts a measurement into another frame or predicts a future state, it applies some function to quantities that are uncertain. The question this post answers is what happens to a Gaussian when you push it through a function.

For a **linear** function the answer is clean: stretching, rotating and shifting a Gaussian gives another Gaussian, and its mean and covariance follow from two short formulas. For a **nonlinear** function the result is generally not Gaussian at all. We then have two practical options.

- **Monte Carlo:** draw many samples, push each one through the function, and summarize the cloud of results. This is accurate given enough samples, but it can be expensive.
- **Linearization:** replace the function by its tangent at the mean, then use the linear formulas. It is cheap and often good, but it fails when the function bends noticeably over the width of the input.

The widget shows both summaries side by side. The **dotted cloud** is Monte Carlo, the **solid ellipse** is a Gaussian fitted to that cloud, and the **dashed ellipse** is the linearized prediction.

## The math

### Linear maps: exact

Let $\mathbf{x} \sim \N(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ and $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ for a fixed matrix $\mathbf{A}$ and vector $\mathbf{b}$. Expectation is linear, so $\E[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}$. The deviation from the mean is $\mathbf{y} - \E[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$, so

$$
\Cov(\mathbf{y}) = \E\!\left[ \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\T\mathbf{A}\T \right] = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}\T .
$$

A linear function of a Gaussian vector is again Gaussian, so

$$
\mathbf{y} \sim \N\!\left( \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b},\ \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}\T \right). \tag{1}
$$

The shift $\mathbf{b}$ moves the mean and leaves the covariance alone. The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ acts on the covariance from both sides, because covariance is a product of two deviations. In one dimension, $\mathbf{A}$ is a number $a$ and the variance is multiplied by $a^2$. Marginalizing, from [Post 3](../conditioning-and-marginalizing/), is the special case where $\mathbf{A}$ just picks out some components.

### Nonlinear maps: Monte Carlo

Now let $\mathbf{y} = f(\mathbf{x})$ for a nonlinear function $f$. In general $\mathbf{y}$ is not Gaussian, and its mean is not $f(\boldsymbol{\mu})$. The most direct way to find out what it is: draw samples $\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}^{(N)}$ from $\N(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ (as in [Post 2](../gaussians-and-covariance/), $\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}$), compute $\mathbf{y}^{(i)} = f(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})$, and work with the outputs. Their sample mean and sample covariance converge to the true mean and covariance of $\mathbf{y}$ as $N$ grows. The error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$, so a hundred times more samples buys one more correct digit. The samples themselves also show the *shape*, which no mean and covariance can.

### Nonlinear maps: linearization

The cheap alternative is to approximate $f$ near the mean by its tangent, the first-order Taylor expansion

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\boldsymbol{\mu}) + \mathbf{J}\,(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}), \qquad \mathbf{J} = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right|_{\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol{\mu}} .
$$

The **Jacobian** $\mathbf{J}$ is the matrix of partial derivatives: entry $(i, j)$ is $\partial f_i / \partial x_j$, so for $f: \R^n \to \R^m$ it is $m \times n$. The approximation is a linear map with $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{J}$, so equation (1) gives

$$
\mathbf{y} \approx \N\!\left( f(\boldsymbol{\mu}),\ \mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{J}\T \right). \tag{2}
$$

This is first-order propagation of uncertainty. It is exactly what the extended Kalman filter (Post 15) does at every step.

For the polar conversion, $f(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ and

$$
\mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} .
$$

At the widget's default reading, $r = 2$ m and $\theta = 90^\circ$, this is $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. With independent errors $\sigma_r = 0.1$ m and $\sigma_\theta = 25^\circ = 0.436$ rad, equation (2) predicts a standard deviation of $2\sigma_\theta = 0.87$ m sideways and $\sigma_r = 0.10$ m along the beam. A bearing error turns into a sideways error proportional to the range.

### What linearization misses

Linearization keeps the slope of $f$ and ignores its curvature. To see what that costs, look at $y = x^2$ with $x \sim \N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, where the exact answer is known:

$$
\E[y] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(y) = 4\mu^2\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^4 .
$$

Linearization, with slope $2\mu$, predicts mean $\mu^2$ and variance $4\mu^2\sigma^2$. It misses the shift $\sigma^2$ in the mean and the term $2\sigma^4$ in the variance. Both come from the curvature, and both grow with the spread $\sigma$. At $\mu = 0$ the slope is zero, and linearization claims $y$ is exactly 0 with no uncertainty at all.

The polar mean has the same flaw. For independent Gaussian $r$ and $\theta$, the exact mean is

$$
\E[(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)] = \mu_r\, e^{-\sigma_\theta^2/2}\,(\cos\mu_\theta,\ \sin\mu_\theta),
$$

pulled towards the sensor by the factor $e^{-\sigma_\theta^2/2}$. With $\sigma_\theta = 25^\circ$ that factor is 0.91, so the mean is at 1.82 m, not at the 2.00 m that linearization reports. Averaging points on an arc gives a point inside the arc.

<Callout type="key">
Linearization is accurate when the function is close to straight over the region where the input has most of its probability. The test is not "is $f$ nonlinear?" but "is $f$ nonlinear on the scale of $\sigma$?"
</Callout>

## Algorithm

<AlgorithmBox title="Propagate N(μ, Σ) through f, two ways" source="src/lib/math/propagate.ts">
1. **Linearized:** evaluate $f(\boldsymbol{\mu})$ and the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ at $\boldsymbol{\mu}$; return $\N(f(\boldsymbol{\mu}), \mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{J}\T)$.
2. **Monte Carlo:** factor $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}\T$; for $i = 1, \ldots, N$ draw $\mathbf{z}^{(i)} \sim \N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ and set $\mathbf{y}^{(i)} = f(\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}^{(i)})$; return the samples and their sample mean and covariance.
</AlgorithmBox>

Every analytic Jacobian in this series is checked in the unit tests against central finite differences, $\partial f_i/\partial x_j \approx \big(f_i(\mathbf{x} + h\mathbf{e}_j) - f_i(\mathbf{x} - h\mathbf{e}_j)\big)/2h$, to a relative error below $10^{-6}$. A wrong Jacobian is one of the most common bugs in estimation code, and it rarely announces itself.

## When it breaks

**Wide inputs and strong curvature.** Linearization degrades when the input spread covers a region where the slope changes a lot. Angles are the usual culprit in robotics: a bearing or heading error of tens of degrees is far outside the region where $\sin$ and $\cos$ look straight. This is also why the fan of dead-reckoned paths in the [first post](../where-am-i/) curves around the loop instead of forming a neat ellipse.

**Flat spots.** Where the slope of $f$ is zero, as for $x^2$ at 0 or $\sin x$ at $\pi/2$, linearization predicts no output uncertainty at all. A filter that believes this becomes overconfident and stops listening to new measurements.

**Linearizing at the wrong point.** Equation (2) uses the Jacobian at the *mean*. If the mean itself is badly wrong, the tangent is taken at the wrong place and the whole approximation can be off. Post 15 shows an extended Kalman filter diverging for exactly this reason.

**The summary is not the distribution.** Even a perfect mean and covariance describe a banana poorly: the fitted ellipse in the widget covers empty space inside the arc and misses the ends. Two better tools come later in the series. The unscented transform (Post 16) chooses a handful of sample points cleverly instead of thousands at random. Post 22 shows that describing pose uncertainty in the right coordinates turns the banana back into an ellipse.

## Summary

- A linear map keeps a Gaussian Gaussian: $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} \sim \N(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}\T)$.
- A nonlinear map generally does not. Monte Carlo sampling shows the true result, at a cost that grows as you ask for more accuracy.
- Linearization uses the Jacobian at the mean: $\N(f(\boldsymbol{\mu}), \mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{J}\T)$. It ignores curvature, so it misplaces the mean and misjudges the spread when the input is wide compared with how quickly $f$ bends.
- Range-and-bearing measurements turn a small bearing error into a large sideways error, and a large one into a banana.
